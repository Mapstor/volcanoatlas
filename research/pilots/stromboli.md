Most volcano pages can give you the date of the last eruption. Stromboli’s can’t, because the eruption hasn’t ended. Below is the record the Smithsonian keeps for it, the numbers behind its height and age, and what its bigger blasts have cost.

> **Stromboli is erupting now.** Its current eruption began on **February 2, 1934**, and it was still going at the Smithsonian’s last update of ongoing eruptions on March 31, 2026. Stromboli is a **stratovolcano** in Italy’s Aeolian Islands. Its summit stands **3,031 ft (924 m)** above the Tyrrhenian Sea.

**Latest report, October 8–14, 2026.** Italy’s INGV reported that eruptive activity continued during October 5–11, with explosions from two vent areas and lava overflowing one vent on October 5–6. The alert level stayed at Yellow, the second lowest of four. [2]

### When did Stromboli last erupt?

It hasn’t stopped. The Smithsonian’s Global Volcanism Program dates the start of the current eruption to **February 2, 1934** and lists it as continuing, which makes it **92 years long** so far. [1]

Only three of the 41 eruptions the Smithsonian lists as continuing began earlier: Yasur in Vanuatu (around 1270), Santa Maria in Guatemala (1922) and Dukono in Indonesia (1933). [1]

The 1934 date hides how steady Stromboli is. The record has it erupting since before 1558, with only four pauses after July 1857. Those pauses add up to **6.5 years out of almost 169**, so Stromboli has been in eruption **96% of the time since 1857**.

### How often does Stromboli erupt?

In its normal state, Stromboli explodes at intervals of **minutes to a few hours**, according to INGV, the Italian institute that monitors it. [3] Each blast lasts from a few seconds to tens of seconds and throws glowing fragments roughly 100 to 1,000 ft (a few tens to hundreds of meters) above the vents.

Bigger events break that rhythm. Lava flows out about **once or twice a decade**; the 2002–2003 flow lasted 206 days. [3] Rarer and far more violent are the blasts INGV calls paroxysms, which throw bombs and blocks as far as the summit area. It names 1919, 1930, 2003 and two in the summer of 2019 as the strongest of the last 100 years.

### What is a Strombolian eruption?

A Strombolian eruption is a string of **short, separate bursts of fluid lava** from a vent or crater, usually basalt. [6] The style takes its name from Stromboli. Each burst throws glowing fragments hundreds of feet into the air, and no sustained ash column forms. [5]

The bursts happen when gas bubbles form in the lava at the vent. [5] You can see what they build without leaving the United States: Strombolian eruptions make cinder cones, and the National Park Service lists Capulin Volcano in New Mexico and Craters of the Moon in Idaho among its examples.

### What type of volcano is Stromboli?

Stromboli is a **stratovolcano**: a steep-sided cone built from lava flows and the debris of explosive eruptions. [7] The Smithsonian lists its main rock as trachyandesite to basaltic trachyandesite and places it above a subduction zone. [1]

The island is only the top of it. The Smithsonian describes Stromboli as the emergent summit of a volcano that grew in two main eruptive cycles. [1] Its active vents sit at the head of the Sciara del Fuoco, a scarp on the northwest side that carries lava and debris downhill and continues below sea level.

### How tall is Stromboli?

Stromboli’s summit is **3,031 ft (924 m) above sea level**. [1] INGV gives about 926 m for the highest point, I Vancori, a difference of 2 m. [3]

That number undersells the volcano. INGV puts its base about 6,600 ft (2,000 m) below sea level, so the whole cone is roughly **9,600 ft (2,900 m) tall, with about two-thirds of it under water**. The active craters aren’t at the top either: they sit on a terrace at about 2,300 to 2,460 ft (700 to 750 m).

### How old is Stromboli?

The part of Stromboli above the sea is **about 85,000 years old**, according to INGV, and volcanic activity in the area began about 200,000 years ago at Strombolicchio. [3]

The Smithsonian’s summary fills in the recent chapters. The Neostromboli period ran from about 13,000 to 5,000 years ago, and the Sciara del Fuoco formed about 5,000 years ago in a series of slope failures. [1] The oldest of the 22 eruptions in its dated record is from around 6,050 BCE.

### Is Stromboli dangerous?

People have died there, though rarely. NOAA’s database of significant eruptions lists **15 events at Stromboli since 1879**. [4] Five were fatal, with **13 deaths in total**.

The deadliest was on September 11, 1930: 6 deaths and 20 injuries. The most recent death was on July 3, 2019.

The sea is the other hazard. NOAA links a tsunami record to **13 of the 15 events**, the latest on July 4, 2024.

### Where is Stromboli?

Stromboli is the northeasternmost of Italy’s Aeolian Islands, in the Tyrrhenian Sea, at 38.789°N, 15.213°E. [1] It is one of 13 volcanoes in [Italy](/volcanoes-in-italy) on the Smithsonian’s Holocene list.

### Stromboli’s full eruption record

The Smithsonian’s record for Stromboli has **22 confirmed eruptions**. It dates 7 of them from the magnetism of old lava flows and one by radiocarbon; the other 14 come from written reports. [1]

### Stromboli questions and answers

**When did Stromboli last erupt?** Stromboli is erupting now. The Smithsonian’s Global Volcanism Program dates its current eruption from February 2, 1934 and listed it as continuing at its last update on March 31, 2026.

**What type of volcano is Stromboli?** Stromboli is a stratovolcano, a steep-sided cone built from lava flows and explosive debris. The Smithsonian lists its main rock type as trachyandesite to basaltic trachyandesite.

**How often does Stromboli erupt?** In normal activity Stromboli explodes at intervals of minutes to a few hours, according to INGV, the Italian institute that monitors it. Lava flows happen about once or twice a decade.

**How tall is Stromboli?** Stromboli’s summit is 3,031 ft (924 m) above sea level. Its base lies about 6,600 ft (2,000 m) below sea level, so the whole volcano is roughly 9,600 ft (2,900 m) tall.

**How old is Stromboli?** The part of Stromboli above the sea is about 85,000 years old, according to INGV. Volcanic activity in the area began about 200,000 years ago at Strombolicchio.

**How long has Stromboli been erupting?** The current eruption has lasted 92 years, since February 2, 1934. The Smithsonian’s summary says mild explosions have been recorded there for more than a millennium.

**Does Stromboli erupt every day?** In normal activity, yes. INGV describes explosions separated by minutes to a few hours, each lasting from a few seconds to tens of seconds.

**Is Stromboli active?** Yes. Stromboli is in continuous eruption, and the Smithsonian / USGS weekly report for October 8–14, 2026 says its activity continued.

**Why is Stromboli called the Lighthouse of the Mediterranean?** Because of its glowing explosions at night, which the Smithsonian says have long attracted visitors.

## How the calculated numbers were made

- **92 years:** from February 2, 1934 to March 31, 2026, the Smithsonian’s last update of ongoing eruptions.
- **96% and 6.5 years:** the days inside the Smithsonian’s eruption dates, divided by all days from July 2, 1857 to March 31, 2026. A pause is the gap between one eruption’s end date and the next start date.
- **9,600 ft (2,900 m):** 924 m above sea level plus about 2,000 m below it. Two-thirds is 2,000 ÷ 2,924.
- **13 deaths:** the sum of NOAA’s totals for the 5 fatal events.
- Feet are converted from meters and rounded.
